Cuban authorities seized more than 4.7 tonnes of drugs between January and
October 2017, most of it marijuana from shipments that washed ashore on the
island's coasts. [@1] The official newspaper Granma said the total exceeded all
the drugs seized in 2015 and in 2016, attributing the rise to Cuba's place on
global trafficking routes and to higher production, supply and consumption. [@1]
Diario de Cuba reported that marijuana wash-ups predominated in 2017 and that
finds of cocaine packages declined. [@2]

The maritime figures came from Operation Aché III, in which members of the
Revolutionary Armed Forces and the Interior Ministry watch Cuba's jurisdictional
waters for drug trafficking. [@1] By the end of October the operation had
confronted 244 wash-up incidents carrying 4,571 kg of drugs, of which 4,462 kg
was marijuana and 109 kg cocaine. [@1] Its personnel sighted 63 speedboats
suspected of involvement in drug trafficking. [@1] The worst-hit areas were the
eastern provinces of Granma and Guantánamo and the southern coast from Pinar del
Río to Camagüey, including the Isla de la Juventud. [@1] Cuban nationals were
detained in the wash-up cases that were clarified. [@1] About 97% of drug
movement in the Caribbean goes by sea, mostly in speedboats passing near Cuban
waters. [@1] Cuban authorities told Granma that tighter regional anti-drug
controls, together with sea currents, push shipments onto the island's coasts.
[@1]

At the air border, 43 international trafficking operations were foiled,
foreigners were detained and 55 kg of drugs were seized. [@1] Havana's José
Martí International Airport was the busiest point for these cases, followed by
the airports of Matanzas and Holguín. [@1] Authorities also detected 46 cases of
foreigners carrying small amounts of drugs for personal use, withdrew the
substances and fined them. [@1] Granma named medicine misuse and marijuana grown
in remote areas as the main domestic problems in the fight against drugs. [@1]
The UNODC's 2017 World Drug Report said trafficking routes were changing and
diversifying at an alarming rate, requiring stronger controls at the maritime
border. [@1]
