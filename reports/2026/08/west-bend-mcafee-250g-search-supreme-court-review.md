West Bend police searched Ayanna McAfee’s car in 2021 after an officer reported
smelling marijuana. [@3] McAfee was riding in the car as a passenger. [@3]
Police had stopped the vehicle for a burned-out headlight, suspended
registration, an arrest warrant for McAfee and her lack of a valid license. [@3]
McAfee subsequently pleaded guilty to possession of THC with intent to deliver
after challenging the search. [@1] The Wisconsin Court of Appeals upheld the
search in October 2025. [@1] The state Supreme Court granted McAfee’s petition
for review to address whether cannabis odor alone provides probable cause to
search a car. [@2] Attorneys for both sides were trading briefs back and forth.
[@3] A date for oral arguments had not yet been set. [@3]

The search turned up 250 grams of marijuana, about $10,000 in cash and a loaded
firearm in the glovebox. [@3] An expert testified that legal hemp CBD and
illegal marijuana produce indistinguishable odors. [@1] The circuit court
accepted that testimony but nevertheless upheld the search. [@1] Washington
County Circuit Court Judge Michael Kenitz ruled the search evidence could be
used. [@3]

The ACLU of Wisconsin filed a 13-page brief for Black Leaders Organizing for
Communities on the case. [@1] The filing asks the court to reconsider State v.
Secrist and the 2023 decision in State v. Moore. [@1] Wisconsin law
distinguishes legal hemp from illegal marijuana by THC concentration that smell
cannot identify. [@1] The filing notes recreational marijuana is legal in
neighboring Illinois, Michigan and Minnesota. [@1] Marijuana odors can remain on
clothing and other surfaces after the substance itself is no longer present.
[@1] The brief cites data that Black Wisconsin residents were 5.29 times more
likely than white residents to be arrested for marijuana possession in 2022
despite comparable use rates. [@1] The state Supreme Court accepted the BLOC
brief on November 18. [@1] The case is numbered 2024AP1635-CR. [@1]
